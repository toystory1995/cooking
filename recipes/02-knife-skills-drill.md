Not a dish — a practice session. Judges notice knife work in the first ten seconds, and
even cuts cook evenly, which is the real reason it matters.

## Ingredients
- 4 carrots, 2 onions, 2 potatoes, 1 celery stick, 1 lemon
- A large bowl (everything you cut becomes stock or soup — nothing is wasted)

## Method
1. **Sharpen first.** A blunt knife slips and needs force, which is how people get cut. If you have a whetstone, ten passes a side at about 15°. Then steel the edge before each session: five light strokes a side, held at a constant angle. A sharp knife should fall through a tomato skin under its own weight.
2. **Grip.** Pinch the blade between thumb and forefinger just in front of the handle — not the handle itself. Your other hand makes a claw: fingertips curled back, knuckles forward against the blade as a guide. The knife tip stays on the board and the blade rocks.
3. **Julienne** the carrots: square off the sides, cut 5cm lengths, slice 2mm planks, stack them, slice 2mm sticks. Aim for matchsticks you could not tell apart.
4. **Brunoise:** turn the julienne 90° and cut 2mm dice. This is the cut in a consommé garnish, and it is unforgiving — every uneven stick shows.
5. **Onion dice:** root end left intact, horizontal cuts, vertical cuts, then across. The root holds the layers together; the size of your final dice is set by the spacing of the first cuts, not the last.
6. **Paysanne, bâtonnet, and a tourné** if you are feeling brave — seven equal faces on a barrel of potato, cut with a small blade, which nobody enjoys and everybody should try.
7. Lay each cut out on a tray and look at them honestly. Then measure a few with a ruler.
8. Everything goes into stock, soup or a mirepoix. Do this session monthly.

## What you're learning
Speed comes from consistency, never the other way round. Also that most kitchen accidents
are a blunt-knife problem or a moving-board problem — put a damp cloth under the board
before you start.

## Push it further
Time a bowl of onion dice and repeat it in a month. Then practise the same cuts on
something soft — a ripe tomato, a mozzarella — where a dull edge has nowhere to hide.
