Twenty minutes to the most useful stock in the world, and a lesson in umami that will
change how you season everything else.

## Ingredients
- 1.5 litres cold water
- 20g kombu (dried kelp)
- 30g katsuobushi (bonito flakes)
- 3 tbsp miso, 100g silken tofu, 2 spring onions, wakame

## Method
1. Wipe the kombu with a dry cloth — do not wash off the white bloom, that is glutamate, the whole point.
2. Soak it in the cold water for 30 minutes if you have time, then bring it slowly towards a simmer. Pull the kombu out **just before boiling**, at around 60–80°C. Boiled kombu turns slimy and bitter, and that is the only real way to fail at this.
3. Bring the water to the boil, take it off the heat, and shower in the katsuobushi. Leave 2 minutes as the flakes sink.
4. Strain through a fine sieve without pressing. You now have *ichiban dashi* — first dashi — clear, pale gold, and tasting of the sea in a way that arrives at the back of the mouth rather than the front.
5. **Miso soup.** Heat dashi to just below a simmer. Add cubed tofu and rehydrated wakame. Slake the miso with a ladle of the hot dashi in a bowl, then stir it back in **off the boil**. Boiling miso kills its aroma and its live cultures. Spring onion on top.
6. Keep the spent kombu and flakes and simmer them again with fresh water for 10 minutes: *niban dashi*, second dashi, which is weaker but perfect for braising vegetables.

## What you're learning
Umami as a technique rather than an ingredient. Kombu gives glutamate, katsuobushi gives
inosinate, and together they are several times more savoury than either alone — the same
synergy behind parmesan on tomato and anchovy in a stew. Also: not everything gets better
with longer cooking.

## Push it further
Use dashi in place of water when you cook rice, and as the braising liquid for the pork
belly. Then make a vegan version with kombu and dried shiitake and taste what changes.
