The sauce that separates cooks who follow recipes from cooks who understand them. It is
held together by nothing but temperature and your attention.

## Ingredients
- 2 fillets of turbot, brill, hake or cod, 150g each
- **Beurre blanc:** 2 shallots finely minced · 60ml white wine vinegar · 60ml dry white wine · 2 tbsp double cream · 200g very cold butter, cubed · salt, white pepper, lemon
- **Poaching:** 500ml light stock or water, a splash of wine, bay leaf, few peppercorns

## Method
1. **Reduction.** Simmer shallots, vinegar and wine in the small pan until only about 2 tablespoons of syrupy liquid remain. Strain if you want a smooth sauce, keep the shallots if you like the texture.
2. Add the cream and bring to a simmer. The cream is insurance — its emulsifiers make the sauce far harder to split, and no classical chef will hold it against you.
3. Drop the heat to its lowest. Whisk in the cold butter **two cubes at a time**, never letting the pan get hotter than you could dip a finger into (below about 60°C). Each addition must melt into the sauce before the next goes in. It will thicken into something the texture of double cream and pale ivory in colour.
4. Season with salt, white pepper and a few drops of lemon. Hold it somewhere barely warm — a bowl over (not in) warm water. If it splits, whisk a fresh tablespoon of cream in a clean pan and dribble the broken sauce into it.
5. **The fish.** Bring the poaching liquid to 70–75°C — steam rising, no bubbles at all. Slide the fillets in and poach 6–8 minutes until the flesh flakes but is still glossy at the centre.
6. Lift out with a slice, drain on a cloth for 10 seconds so water does not thin your sauce, and plate on a pool of beurre blanc.

## What you're learning
A temperature-held emulsion: butter is roughly 80% fat and 16% water, and whisking it in
cold and slowly keeps the fat suspended in the reduction rather than melting out into oil.
Everything you know about vinaigrette applies here with a much narrower margin.

## Push it further
Split the finished sauce on purpose over higher heat, then rescue it. Do it twice. Knowing
the failure by feel is worth more than three successful attempts.
