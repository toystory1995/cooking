Four ingredients and one judgement: how much gelatine. Too little and it collapses; too much
and it bounces. The correct answer is *barely set* — it should wobble alarmingly and hold
its shape for about three seconds after you turn it out.

## Ingredients
- 400ml double cream
- 100ml whole milk
- 60g caster sugar
- 1 vanilla pod, split (or a strip of lemon zest, a bay leaf, coffee beans, basil)
- **3g gelatine** — 1½ leaves, or ¾ tsp powder
- Neutral oil for the moulds
- To serve: macerated berries, poached rhubarb, or a spoonful of good honey

## Method
1. **Bloom the gelatine.** Leaves go into cold water for 5 minutes until floppy, then squeezed out. Powder is sprinkled over 3 tablespoons of cold water and left to swell. Gelatine added dry to hot liquid makes strings that never dissolve.
2. Heat the cream, milk, sugar and vanilla to about 80°C — steaming, not boiling. Boiling gelatine weakens its setting power, and boiled cream tastes cooked.
3. Off the heat, stir in the bloomed gelatine until completely dissolved. Rub a little between your fingers to check for grains.
4. Infuse for 20 minutes if using anything other than vanilla, then strain. Cool to room temperature, stirring occasionally so the vanilla seeds stay suspended rather than sinking.
5. Pour into lightly oiled moulds or straight into glasses. Chill at least **4 hours**, ideally overnight.
6. **Turn out:** dip the mould in hot water for 3 seconds, press one edge to break the seal, and invert onto the plate with a firm shake.

## The ratio, which is the actual lesson
Per **500ml** of liquid:
- **2–3g gelatine** — barely set, spoonable, for glasses. This is a proper panna cotta.
- **5–6g** — firm enough to turn out and hold a clean edge.
- **8–10g** — a firm jelly. Too firm for cream, right for a fruit jelly layer.
- **12g+** — rubber. This is what most restaurant panna cotta is, and it is wrong.

Agar behaves completely differently: about **2g per 500ml**, it must *boil* to activate, it
sets at room temperature and gives a brittle, sliceable set rather than a melting one.
Gelatine melts in the mouth at body temperature; agar does not, which is why one feels
luxurious and the other feels clean.

## What you're learning
Setting agents as a dial rather than a yes-or-no. This ratio table is the same knowledge
you will use for the entremet at level 5, for aspic in soup dumplings, and for jellies,
mousses and terrines — most of which fail for exactly this reason.

## Push it further
Make three at 2g, 5g and 10g in the same session and taste them side by side. Then do a
buttermilk panna cotta, which is sharper and needs a touch more gelatine because of the acid.
