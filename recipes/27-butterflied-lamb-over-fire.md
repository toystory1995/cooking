Live fire is its own discipline — no dial, no timer, just fuel, distance and time. Every
competition eventually puts a cook in front of one.

## Ingredients
- 1 leg of lamb, 2–2.5kg, bone in (you will remove it)
- **Marinade:** 6 cloves garlic crushed · 3 tbsp olive oil · zest and juice of 1 lemon · 2 tbsp chopped rosemary · 1 tbsp oregano · 2 tsp salt · 1 tsp cracked pepper
- To serve: yoghurt with mint and garlic

## Method
1. **Butterfly.** Sit the leg fat-side down. Cut along the length of the bone until you hit it, then work the meat away from the bone with the tip of the knife, opening the leg out like a book. Take out the aitch bone at the wide end by feeling around it. Then score the thick parts and open them out so the whole thing is a roughly even 4cm thick — evenness matters more than neatness.
2. Marinate at least 4 hours, ideally overnight. Bring to room temperature an hour before cooking.
3. **Build a two-zone fire.** Bank all the coals to one side, leaving the other half of the grill empty. The hot side sears; the cool side cooks. Without this you have one setting and it is *burnt*.
4. Wait for the coals to stop flaming and go grey-ashed with a red glow beneath. Hold your hand 12cm above: **three seconds** of tolerable heat is your searing zone.
5. Sear the lamb fat-side down over the coals, 4–5 minutes, moving it whenever fat drips and flares. Flames lick soot onto the meat; embers cook it. Turn and sear the other side.
6. Move to the cool side, cover if you have a lid, and cook 20–30 minutes, turning every 8, until the thickest part reads **54°C** for pink. The thin end will be more done — that is a feature, not a fault, and it feeds people who like it that way.
7. Rest 15 minutes on a board. Carve **across the grain**, which changes direction across a butterflied leg, so look at the meat before each cut rather than slicing straight down the board.

## What you're learning
Reading heat without a number: coal colour, hand distance, and the sound of fat hitting
embers. Also that fire cooking is managed by *moving the food*, not by adjusting the fire.

## Push it further
Cook a whole fish over the same fire in a grill basket, and a head of hispi cabbage buried
in the embers. Learning what the dying fire is still good for is half of it.
