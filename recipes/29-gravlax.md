Salt, sugar, time. Curing is preservation, but it is also seasoning and texture, and it
gives you a component you can make days ahead — which matters enormously under time
pressure.

## Ingredients
- 1 side of salmon, 800g–1kg, pin-boned, skin on
- 150g coarse salt
- 100g sugar
- 2 tbsp crushed white peppercorns
- Large bunch of dill, chopped
- Optional: 50ml aquavit or gin, zest of a lemon, 2 tbsp beetroot grated (for colour)
- **Mustard sauce:** 2 tbsp Dijon · 1 tbsp sugar · 1 tbsp white wine vinegar · 100ml oil · dill
- 4 egg yolks and extra salt/sugar mix, for curing yolks

## Method
1. Mix salt, sugar and pepper. This 3:2 ratio is the classic; more salt cures faster and firmer, more sugar keeps it softer.
2. Lay half the cure in a tray, sit the salmon on it skin-side down, and pack the rest over the flesh with the dill and any aromatics. Wrap tightly.
3. Weight it with a board and a few tins and refrigerate **24–48 hours**, turning once a day and pouring off the brine that collects. That liquid is water drawn out of the fish by osmosis — it is why the flesh firms up and why it keeps.
4. At 24 hours it is lightly cured and silky; at 48 it is firm and salty. Rinse, pat dry, and rest uncovered in the fridge for a few hours to form a slight tack on the surface.
5. **Slice** on a shallow angle with a long knife, in single strokes, as thin as you can manage, leaving the skin behind.
6. **Cured yolks, alongside.** Bury whole yolks in a 50/50 salt and sugar mix in a small tub, cover, and refrigerate 4 days until firm like a soft sweet. Rinse, dry in a very low oven or dehydrator for 2 hours, then grate over the gravlax like bottarga.
7. Whisk the mustard sauce and serve with rye bread.

## What you're learning
Water activity — the reason curing preserves is that salt makes water unavailable to
microbes. The same principle underlies bottarga, prosciutto and preserved lemons, and it
gives you make-ahead components with real depth.

## Push it further
Cure a piece with beetroot and gin for colour, and try a fast 6-hour cure on thin fillets.
Then use offcuts in a tartare and taste how curing changed the texture.
