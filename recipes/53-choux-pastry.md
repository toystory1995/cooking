The only pastry cooked twice — once on the hob, once in the oven — and one where you must
stop adding egg by feel rather than by count.

## Ingredients
- 125ml water, 125ml whole milk
- 100g butter
- 1 tsp sugar, ½ tsp salt
- 150g strong plain flour, sifted
- 4–5 eggs, beaten
- **Craquelin (optional but transformative):** 50g soft butter · 60g brown sugar · 60g flour, rolled thin between paper and frozen
- Crème pâtissière (recipe 55), or whipped cream and chocolate sauce

## Method
1. Bring the water, milk, butter, sugar and salt to a **full rolling boil** — the butter must be completely melted before it boils, so start it in cold liquid.
2. Off the heat, add all the flour at once and beat hard until it forms a ball.
3. **Dry the panade**: back on medium heat, beat and spread the dough against the pan for 2–3 minutes until a floury film forms on the base and it stops sticking to your spoon. Drying it out here is what lets it absorb enough egg.
4. Cool to about 60°C — beat it in a bowl for a minute to help. Then add the beaten egg **a little at a time**, beating fully between additions.
5. **Stop when the consistency is right, not when the eggs are gone.** The paste should fall from the spoon in a thick V-shaped ribbon after about three seconds, and a trench drawn through it should slowly close. Too little egg gives dense, cracked buns; too much and they spread and never rise.
6. Pipe: 4cm rounds for profiteroles, 12cm lengths for éclairs, well spaced. Flatten any peaks with a wet fingertip. Top with a disc of frozen craquelin if using — it gives an even crackled dome.
7. Bake at 200°C for 10 minutes, then **without opening the door** drop to 175°C for 20–25 minutes until deeply golden and light in the hand. Steam from the liquid inflates them; the shell must set before you let that steam out.
8. Pierce each one underneath and return to the switched-off oven for 10 minutes to dry the insides.
9. Cool completely, fill through the hole with a piping bag, glaze or dip.

## What you're learning
Judging a batter by consistency, which is the honest way to bake — the number of eggs
depends on your flour, your pan and how long you dried the panade. Also that steam is a
raising agent in its own right, as it was in the croissants and the flatbread.

## Push it further
Same paste: gougères with cheese, churros fried instead of baked, and a Paris-Brest with
praline mousseline — which is a genuine competition-standard dessert.
